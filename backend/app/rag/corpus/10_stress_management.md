# Stress Management

## Overview
Stress is the body's response to perceived demands or threats. Acute stress (short-term, tied to a specific event) is normal and can even be motivating, but chronic stress — sustained activation of the body's stress response over weeks or months — is linked to physical health problems (cardiovascular strain, weakened immune function, digestive issues) and mental health difficulties (anxiety, depression, burnout).

## Common Sources
Work demands, financial pressure, relationship conflict, caregiving responsibilities, major life transitions, and chronic uncertainty are common stress sources. Notably, both negative and positive major life changes (a new job, a move, a wedding) can be stressful because they require adaptation.

## Supportive Approaches
- **Identifying controllables vs. uncontrollables**: Directing problem-solving energy toward what can actually be influenced, and practicing acceptance strategies for what cannot.
- **Physical regulation**: Regular movement, adequate sleep, and reducing stimulants (excess caffeine) can meaningfully reduce baseline stress reactivity.
- **Boundary setting**: Learning to say no to additional demands when already at capacity is a core and often underused stress management skill.
- **Micro-recovery**: Brief regular breaks throughout the day (even 5-10 minutes) prevent the kind of sustained stress buildup that leads to burnout, more effectively than occasional long breaks alone.
- **Social support**: Talking through stressors with trusted others reduces the physiological stress response, a well-documented effect sometimes called "social buffering."

## Burnout Note
Burnout (emotional exhaustion, cynicism/detachment, reduced sense of accomplishment, typically tied to chronic workplace stress) is distinct from general stress and often requires structural changes, not just individual coping strategies, to fully resolve.
