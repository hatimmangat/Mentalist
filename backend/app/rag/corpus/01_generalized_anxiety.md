# Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

## Overview
Generalized Anxiety Disorder is characterized by persistent, excessive worry about multiple areas of life (work, health, relationships, finances) that is difficult to control and occurs more days than not for at least six months. Unlike situational stress, GAD worry tends to be free-floating and can shift rapidly between topics.

## Common Symptoms
Restlessness or feeling on edge, fatigue, difficulty concentrating or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance (trouble falling or staying asleep, or unsatisfying sleep). Physical symptoms like a racing heart, sweating, or gastrointestinal discomfort are common because anxiety activates the body's stress response even without an external threat.

## Cognitive Patterns
People with GAD often engage in "what-if" thinking, catastrophizing (assuming the worst-case outcome), and intolerance of uncertainty — a strong discomfort with not knowing how something will turn out, which drives compulsive worry as a (ineffective) attempt to feel prepared.

## Supportive Approaches
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is the most evidence-supported approach, particularly techniques that target intolerance of uncertainty and worry postponement (scheduling a specific "worry time" rather than worrying throughout the day). Grounding techniques, diaphragmatic breathing, and progressive muscle relaxation can reduce physiological arousal in the moment. Structured problem-solving (distinguishing solvable worries from hypothetical ones) is also effective.

## When to Encourage Professional Support
If worry is persistent, distressing, interfering with daily functioning, or accompanied by panic attacks, avoidance behaviors, or significant sleep loss, encourage the person to speak with a licensed mental health professional or physician for a full evaluation. This information is educational and does not replace clinical diagnosis.
