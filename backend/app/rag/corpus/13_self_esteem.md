# Self-Esteem and Self-Compassion

## Overview
Self-esteem refers to a person's overall sense of self-worth. Low self-esteem often develops from early critical or inconsistent relationships, chronic comparison to others, perfectionistic standards, or internalizing harsh feedback. It commonly underlies or co-occurs with anxiety, depression, and relationship difficulties.

## Self-Esteem vs. Self-Compassion
Traditional self-esteem work focuses on evidence of competence and worth, which can become contingent on constant achievement. Self-compassion, an increasingly well-supported alternative framework (developed prominently by researcher Kristin Neff), focuses on treating oneself with the same kindness one would offer a struggling friend, regardless of performance, and has three components: self-kindness (vs. self-judgment), common humanity (recognizing struggle as part of the shared human experience, not a personal failing), and mindfulness (holding difficult feelings without over-identifying with them).

## Common Unhelpful Patterns
Harsh internal self-talk, perfectionism (treating any imperfection as failure), comparison to curated versions of others' lives, and the "imposter phenomenon" (persistent feeling of being a fraud despite evidence of competence).

## Supportive Approaches
- Noticing and gently challenging harsh self-talk, comparing it to how one would speak to a friend in the same situation.
- Separating self-worth from performance or productivity.
- Building a realistic, balanced self-view rather than swinging between harsh self-criticism and forced positivity.
- Practicing self-compassion specifically in moments of failure or struggle, which research suggests is more motivating long-term than self-criticism.

## When to Encourage Professional Support
Persistent low self-worth significantly affecting relationships, work, or accompanied by depressive symptoms warrants professional support.
