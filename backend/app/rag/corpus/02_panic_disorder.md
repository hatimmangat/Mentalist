# Panic Disorder and Panic Attacks

## Overview
A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes, involving physical symptoms such as heart palpitations, chest tightness, shortness of breath, trembling, dizziness, sweating, and a sense of unreality (derealization) or detachment from oneself (depersonalization). Panic Disorder involves recurrent, unexpected panic attacks followed by persistent worry about having more attacks or their consequences.

## The Fear Cycle
Panic often becomes self-reinforcing: physical sensations (like a fast heartbeat) are misinterpreted as dangerous ("I'm having a heart attack" or "I'm losing control"), which increases fear, which increases physical arousal, creating a feedback loop. Many people begin avoiding places or situations associated with past attacks, which can narrow their world over time (a pattern seen in panic disorder with agoraphobia).

## Supportive Approaches
Psychoeducation about the biology of panic (that it is intensely uncomfortable but not medically dangerous) is often the first and most powerful intervention. Slow diaphragmatic breathing, grounding techniques (such as the 5-4-3-2-1 sensory technique), and interoceptive exposure (gradually and safely getting used to the physical sensations of arousal) delivered through CBT are well-supported treatments.

## Immediate, In-the-Moment Support
If someone describes being in the middle of a panic attack, prioritize calm, simple language: encourage slow exhale-focused breathing, remind them the sensations will pass, and avoid overwhelming them with too much information at once. Do not dismiss the experience as "just anxiety" — it is genuinely distressing.

## When to Encourage Professional Support
Recurring panic attacks, especially with avoidance behavior or fear between episodes, warrant a conversation with a licensed clinician. Chest pain or symptoms resembling a cardiac event should always be evaluated by a medical professional first to rule out physical causes.
