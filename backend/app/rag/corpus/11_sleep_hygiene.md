# Sleep and Mental Health

## Overview
Sleep and mental health have a bidirectional relationship: poor sleep worsens anxiety, depression, and emotional regulation, and psychological distress often disrupts sleep. Addressing sleep is frequently one of the highest-leverage supportive interventions because of this two-way effect.

## Common Sleep Disruptors in Mental Distress
Racing thoughts or worry at bedtime, using the bed for non-sleep activities (scrolling phones, working), irregular sleep-wake schedules, caffeine or alcohol close to bedtime, and using sleep avoidance as an anxiety-driven behavior (staying up to avoid nightmares or intrusive thoughts, seen in trauma).

## Sleep Hygiene Principles
- Consistent sleep and wake times, even on weekends, to stabilize the body's circadian rhythm.
- A wind-down routine in the 30-60 minutes before bed, away from screens where possible.
- Reserving the bed for sleep (and intimacy) rather than work, scrolling, or worrying, to strengthen the mental association between bed and sleep.
- Limiting caffeine after early afternoon and reducing alcohol close to bedtime (alcohol can help initiate sleep but fragments it later in the night).
- If unable to sleep after roughly 20 minutes, getting up and doing something calm in low light rather than lying in bed frustrated, which can condition anxiety around the bed itself.

## When to Encourage Professional Support
Persistent insomnia (more than a few weeks), suspected sleep apnea (loud snoring, gasping, excessive daytime sleepiness), or sleep disruption tied to significant mental health symptoms warrants evaluation by a physician or sleep specialist alongside any mental health support.
