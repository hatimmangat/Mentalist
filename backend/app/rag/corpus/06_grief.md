# Grief and Bereavement

## Overview
Grief is the natural response to loss — most commonly death, but also applicable to loss of relationships, health, identity, or major life expectations. Grief is not a linear process; the popularized "five stages" (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) are not a fixed sequence everyone follows, and modern grief research emphasizes that grieving looks different for every person and can involve waves of intensity rather than a straight path.

## Common Experiences
Intense sadness, yearning for the person or thing lost, intrusive memories, difficulty accepting the loss, anger, guilt (including "survivor's guilt" or regret over things said or unsaid), numbness, and physical symptoms like fatigue or appetite change. Grief can also involve moments of relief or even laughter, which people sometimes feel guilty about — this is a normal part of the process, not a sign of not caring.

## Complicated or Prolonged Grief
While there is no fixed timeline for grief, when intense grief symptoms persist beyond twelve months (in adults) with significant functional impairment, longing, or an inability to accept the loss, it may be classified as Prolonged Grief Disorder and can benefit from specialized grief-focused therapy.

## Supportive Approaches
The most helpful support is often simply presence and validation rather than trying to "fix" the pain or rush someone toward acceptance. Avoid clichés that minimize the loss ("everything happens for a reason," "at least..."). Encourage the person to talk about the person or thing they lost if they want to, and gently normalize that grief can resurface around anniversaries, holidays, or unexpected triggers long after the loss.

## When to Encourage Professional Support
If grief is accompanied by thoughts of self-harm, complete inability to function, or symptoms consistent with prolonged grief disorder, encourage connecting with a grief counselor or therapist.
